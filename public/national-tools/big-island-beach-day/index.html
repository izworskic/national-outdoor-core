How the call is made

The rules, published

No score and no hidden weighting. Each coast gets one of three steps, from the worst signal available.

  1. An active High Surf Advisory or Warning for that coast is a rough day, full stop. The National Weather Service decides this, not the tool.
  2. The NWS Surf Zone Forecast gives a range in feet for each facing shore. A high end of 4 feet or more is moderate and 7 feet or more is large.
  3. The wave model (PacIOOS SWAN) is compared with that coast’s own record: the daytime peak height on every day from 2016 to 2025. Bigger than 70 percent of those days is moderate and bigger than 90 percent is large. This is why the model is not read in meters: the same height means different things on a sheltered Kona cove and an open Kohala beach.
  4. Long-period swell and onshore wind nudge a calm day up to moderate. Swell of 14 seconds or more throws surge and shore break that its height understates, and wind blowing onto the shore above 15 mph chops the surface.
  5. If the forecast and the model are two steps apart the answer is CHECK, not a guess.

Honest limit: the model alone is a weak judge of danger. Checked against ten years of NWS advisories, the daily peak height separated advisory days from other days only modestly (about 0.76 on Kohala and 0.63 on the south coast, where 0.5 is a coin flip and 1.0 is perfect). That is why the NWS forecast and alerts lead and the model is read against each coast’s own history. The step cutoffs are starting rules and confidence never rises above MODERATE. Brown water after heavy rain, jellyfish and vog are not part of this call.

The questions visitors actually ask

Reading a Big Island beach day

Why is Kona so often calm?

The Kona and Kohala coasts sit in the island’s lee. Mauna Kea, Mauna Loa and Hualalai block the trade-wind swell that hits Hilo and Puna, so the west side is calm for much of the year. Winter north and west swells are the exception, especially on Kohala and North Kona.

What does a high surf advisory mean for me?

It means large breaking waves and strong currents that can sweep people off the shore. Beach parks often close their water access. Treat it as a day to stay out of the water, even on a beach that looks fine from the sand.

Is the calm side always the safe side?

No. Calm water can still have rip currents, and a shore break can be dangerous when the height looks small if the swell is long-period. Read the notes on each coast, and swim near a lifeguard where there is one.

Why does a coast say CHECK?

The two main sources disagree by two steps, for example the forecast says small and the model says big for that coast. Rather than pick one, the tool tells you to judge the water yourself.

How far ahead is this good?

The NWS surf forecast covers about two days and the wave model about four. Days past the forecast rely on the model alone and the page says so.

What about brown water and vog?

Not in the call. After heavy rain, runoff can make water unsafe near streams and the Hawaii Department of Health posts advisories. Check them before you swim after a storm.
